Proof of what a learner can really do — not just a mark on a page.

Taalo is a school app built around one honest question: can this child reliably do this on their own, in more than one situation, right now? Not "what percentage did they score on Tuesday." This page walks through what that means in everyday terms, how Taalo works underneath, and how you can see it for yourself.

The problem

Most school apps answer the wrong question.

Kenya's Competency-Based Curriculum (CBC) was built to move away from "how many marks out of 100." It asks something more specific and more useful — and most digital tools quietly answer a different question instead, without anyone deciding to make that swap.

"Can this learner reliably do this, unaided, in more than one setting, right now?"

That's the CBC question. Here's the question most software actually ends up answering:

"What fraction of the marks on this one task did they get right?"

The screen might still say "competency," and the report might still say "Meeting Expectation" — but underneath, it's just arithmetic on marks, not a real judgement about ability. It happens because marks are the only thing the software ever collects, so marks become the only thing it can ever report on. Taalo was built to close that specific gap: what gets measured, on every single screen, changes from a score on a task to a piece of evidence about an ability — dated, put in context, and tagged with how independently the learner produced it.

Why averages mislead

Why "just average the scores" can't work.

Averaging feels fair and simple. It's also the reason so many "competency" reports don't actually mean anything. Four everyday reasons why:

  1. 01Averages hide direction. Picture two learners. One scores 40%, then 60%, then 90% — clearly improving. The other scores 90%, then 60%, then 40% — clearly slipping. Average both, and they land on the exact same number. An average can't tell you which learner is thriving and which one needs help right now.
  2. 02One good task isn't proof. A learner who gets a worksheet right on counting money hasn't necessarily proven they can count real coins at the market. A single piece of work, even a perfect one, shouldn't be enough to declare "mastered."
  3. 03A right answer isn't always an independent answer. A correct response a learner reached with heavy teacher help is not the same achievement as one they reached completely alone. A percentage can't tell those two apart — Taalo records the difference on purpose.
  4. 04Not everything CBC cares about is a score. CBC's own goals include things like "enjoys sorting and grouping objects" — an attitude, not a mark. You can't average your way to judging that kind of thing.
How Taalo decides

Four simple rules, applied to every learner, every time.

Instead of averaging, Taalo treats every test question, assignment, and teacher observation as one dated note in a learner's file — evidence, never just a number. A learner's current level for a competency is worked out fresh from that evidence, using four rules:

1

Every piece of work is judged on its own.

A test question, an assignment question, a moment a teacher observes — each one gets its own honest judgement (below, approaching, meeting, or exceeding expectation). Nothing gets blended into one score before it's judged, so nothing gets watered down.

2

What's recent matters most.

A learner's status is based on their most recent handful of results for that skill — not everything they've ever done. Older results still sit in their history for the timeline view, but they stop dragging today's status up or down. If a learner has grown, their record should say so.

3

How much help was needed matters too.

Every piece of evidence notes whether the learner worked independently, with some guidance, or with heavy support. A perfect score with heavy support shows the learner is engaged — it does not show mastery, and Taalo won't label it "meeting expectation" no matter how high the mark was.

4

One good day, in one setting, is never quite enough.

To be marked "meeting" or "exceeding," a learner needs evidence from at least two different settings somewhere in their history — say, a written test and a classroom observation. That's proof the skill transfers beyond one lucky test paper, which is the entire point of a competency claim.

A learner's status can move up or down as new evidence comes in — there's no floor once a level is reached, and no button for a teacher to quietly override it. A teacher records what happened. Taalo works out what it means.

Where the evidence comes from

Three ways a teacher records what happened — one shared learner record.

Whether a teacher watches a learner do something, marks an assignment, or grades a test, it all lands in the same place and gets read by the same set of rules.

Observation

Teacher watches the learner do it in class and records a simple fact: did they show it, or not yet — plus how much help they needed.

Assignment

Learner works through take-home or classwork questions. Each question is marked right or wrong on its own — not blended into one big grade.

Test

Same careful, question-by-question marking as an assignment — a formal test just adds a different setting to the learner's evidence trail.

One up-to-date learner record

Taalo doesn't know or care which of the three a piece of evidence came from — it just applies the same four rules across everything. That's what makes "two different settings" a meaningful test of real mastery, instead of a technicality.

The difference it makes

Grade tracking vs. competence tracking.

The old way
  • A learner's grade is a lifetime average of every score they've ever gotten.
  • One good test can hide months of struggling — or one bad day can bury months of real progress.
  • A right answer counts the same whether the learner did it alone or with heavy help.
  • "Competency" is really just a percentage wearing a new label.
  • Improvement and decline can look identical on the report.
The Taalo way
  • A learner's status is read fresh from their most recent evidence — not a lifetime average.
  • Older results stay visible in the timeline but stop pulling today's status toward them.
  • How independently the work was done caps how high it can count.
  • "Meeting expectation" requires proof from more than one real setting.
  • Trajectory — getting better or slipping — is visible, not hidden.
Built honestly

We'd rather tell you what's still being improved.

Taalo is a live product, and we're upfront about where it's still growing rather than pretending it's finished.

Test and assignment questions are tagged by topic area for now, not the exact skill inside it.

A topic area can cover a few related skills at once, so this evidence currently lands in that broader pool rather than one named skill — until questions are tagged that precisely. That's a decision for the screens where teachers build tests and assignments, and it's next on the list.

Right now, every question in one assignment shares the same "setting" tag.

So the two-settings rule mostly tells apart "this was tested" from "this was observed in class" — not different real-world situations within a test itself. We'll revisit this once there's a clear need for finer detail.

Questions people ask

Common questions, answered simply.

If my child does badly once, does their record drop immediately?
One result is never the whole story — status is read from the last handful of results, so a single rough day is put into context alongside everything recent, not treated as the final word.
Can a teacher just mark a child as "meeting expectation" directly?
No. Teachers record what actually happened — a test result, an assignment, or what they observed. Taalo works out the status from that evidence. There's no manual override, so the status always reflects real evidence.
Does old work get deleted once a learner improves?
No — every piece of evidence stays in the learner's history and timeline forever. It just stops being the main thing that decides today's status once it's no longer recent.
What does "independent, guided, or with heavy support" actually mean day to day?
It's simply how the teacher records whether the learner worked it out alone, needed some hints along the way, or needed close, hands-on help. It's a normal part of recording the result — not extra paperwork.
Is Taalo only for teachers, or can parents see it too?
Parents get visibility into what their child can actually and reliably do — not just a mark on one test — so progress (or a dip that needs attention) is easy to see and talk about at home.
